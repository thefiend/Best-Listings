Singapore has one of the most serious Japanese restaurant scenes outside Japan. The density of Michelin-starred sushi counters, the depth of the omakase culture, and the willingness of Singapore diners to pay for genuine quality have created an environment where world-class sushi chefs choose to open here ahead of other major Asian capitals. We visited over 35 sushi restaurants across the island — from two-Michelin-star omakase destinations to mid-range sushi bars and casual kaiten operations — ranking each on fish freshness and provenance, rice seasoning and temperature, chef technique and knife work, atmosphere and service, and value relative to the experience delivered. This list covers the full spectrum, from the finest Edomae counters to the best budget options, so that every level of sushi interest has a clear recommendation.

**Key Takeaways**

- **Best overall:** Shinji by Kanesaka at Carlton Hotel and St Regis, 2 Michelin stars, the most authentic Edo-style sushi counter in Southeast Asia
- **Best omakase value:** Teppei Japanese Restaurant on Club Street, beloved for accessible omakase at multiple price points that punch well above their cost
- **Best single chef experience:** Sushi Kimura at Mandarin Gallery, 1 Michelin star, where Chef Kimura's Edomae precision is apparent in every nigiri
- **Best kaiten:** Genki Sushi at multiple outlets, a reliable, well-run conveyor belt operation with the widest selection at the lowest per-plate price
- **Most creative:** Mitzo at Grand Park Orchard, where modern Japanese-Cantonese fusion produces sushi rolls unlike anything at a traditional counter
- Omakase dining in Singapore typically costs SGD 150–350 per person for a full seasonal menu at a Michelin-recognised restaurant; mid-tier omakase at Teppei and Tatsuya runs SGD 80–150; kaiten sushi at Genki and Sakae costs SGD 20–50 per person
- Advance booking is essential at Shinji by Kanesaka, Shoukouwa, and Sushi Kimura — seats at peak dining times are reserved weeks ahead; walk-ins succeed most reliably at the casual end of this list

## How We Ranked These Sushi Restaurants

We evaluated every restaurant across five criteria weighted to reflect what actually separates great sushi from merely acceptable sushi. Fish freshness and provenance carried the most weight — the source, handling, and ageing of the fish is the factor no amount of technique can compensate for if it is wrong. Rice seasoning and temperature was evaluated independently, because properly prepared shari (sushi rice) seasoned with the correct rice vinegar blend and served at the precise body-temperature warmth is as demanding a technical discipline as the fish preparation itself. Chef technique covered knife skill, nigiri formation pressure, and the consistency of execution across the full menu. Atmosphere and service acknowledged that the dining environment shapes the experience at a sushi counter in ways that do not apply to ramen or hawker food. Value was assessed relative to the experience delivered at each price tier rather than as an absolute comparison across radically different restaurant types. Kaiten and casual chains were evaluated on their own terms — consistency, selection, and value per plate — not against Michelin-star omakase standards.

### 1. Shinji by Kanesaka — Singapore's Finest Edo-Style Sushi

Shinji by Kanesaka holds two Michelin stars across its Carlton Hotel and St Regis outlets and remains the clearest expression of authentic Edomae sushi available in Singapore or, arguably, in any Southeast Asian city. The restaurant is an extension of the Kanesaka lineage in Tokyo — a sushi house that trained its chefs in the Edo tradition, where every element of the meal from the shari temperature to the ageing time of each fish is determined by accumulated technical knowledge rather than intuition or approximation. The nigiri at Shinji is formed with a deliberately light pressure that creates a structure airy enough to fall apart gently on the palate rather than presenting as a compact block of rice with fish draped over it — a distinction that separates authentic Edomae technique from the imitative versions that populate lesser counters. The fish selection rotates with Japanese seasonal availability, sourced through premium channels that give the kitchen access to the same market-quality product that the Tokyo original works with. At 4.5 stars, the sustained quality across both Singapore outlets over multiple years of operation is the most reliable endorsement available for a two-Michelin-star sushi counter in this market.

## Omakase vs À la Carte Sushi — Which is Worth It in Singapore?

The omakase format — in which the chef selects every course based on the day's best produce and the diner's dietary requirements — has become the dominant framework for serious Japanese dining in Singapore, and for good reason. At a genuine omakase counter, the chef's ability to sequence courses, control portion sizes, and build flavour progressions across a full meal produces an experience that à la carte ordering cannot replicate regardless of how good the individual dishes are. The rhythm of an omakase meal — light tsumami appetisers, grilled and braised intermediary courses, the nigiri sequence that forms the centrepiece, and the lighter closing items — is a composed structure rather than a collection of individual choices, and the composed structure is almost always superior to the assembled alternative.

For diners with specific dietary preferences or strong opinions about particular fish species, à la carte sushi at a quality restaurant like Sushi Kong or Tatsuya gives control that the omakase format does not. Diners who know they want a large quantity of salmon sashimi and nothing else are better served by à la carte ordering than by a curated multi-course omakase where the chef determines the balance of proteins and preparations.

The practical answer in Singapore is to reserve omakase for occasions where the full experience — the counter interaction, the chef's judgement, the progressive sequence — adds value, and to use à la carte for more casual, appetite-driven visits. Both formats are well represented across this list at multiple price points.

## How to Book and What to Expect at a Singapore Sushi Omakase

**Booking.** At Shinji by Kanesaka and Shoukouwa, book through the restaurant's website or by phone at least two to three weeks in advance for weekend seatings; weekday lunch is more accessible and worth exploring if scheduling allows. Sushi Kimura and Teppei require two to four weeks advance booking for popular evening slots. Tatsuya and Sushi Jiro are generally more accessible with one to two weeks notice. Cancellations do occur — calling on the day or the day before occasionally produces last-minute seating at even the most sought-after counters.

**What to communicate when booking.** Inform the restaurant of any dietary restrictions, allergies, or foods you cannot eat at the time of booking rather than at the counter. Omakase menus are prepared sequentially and ingredient substitutions are more manageable when the chef has advance notice. At Michelin-level counters, communicating preferred price tier — most offer two or three omakase menus at different price points — at booking ensures the correct mise en place is prepared for your seating.

**Counter etiquette.** At a sushi counter, nigiri should be eaten immediately after it is placed in front of you — the rice temperature and the fish temperature are calibrated for immediate consumption and both deteriorate within minutes. Eating with fingers rather than chopsticks is traditional and acceptable at all counters on this list. Avoid heavy perfume or cologne at an intimate counter, as fragrance interferes with the olfactory experience for both you and the diners seated beside you.

**Beverage pairing.** Sake pairings are available at Shinji, Shoukouwa, and Sushi Kimura and are worth considering for a full omakase experience — the pairing improves the quality of the evening significantly if sake is within your comfort zone. Beer and non-alcoholic pairings are available at all restaurants. At Teppei and Tatsuya, the value positioning of the food makes a considered sake selection a proportionally larger quality improvement to the overall bill.

### What is omakase?

Omakase is a Japanese dining format in which the diner entrusts the meal entirely to the chef — the word translates directly as "I leave it to you." Rather than ordering from a menu, the chef selects every course based on the day's best seasonal produce, the guest's dietary requirements, and the intended progression of the meal. In Singapore's sushi context, omakase typically involves a sequence of tsumami (small appetiser courses), followed by a series of individually served nigiri sushi pieces, each explained by the chef as it is presented. The format originated at the high end of Tokyo's sushi culture and has become the dominant framework for serious Japanese dining in Singapore.

### How much does omakase cost in Singapore?

For casual sushi at Sushi Kong, Genki Sushi, and Sakae Sushi, walk-ins are the norm and reservations are not required.

### What is Edomae sushi?

Edomae sushi is the style of sushi that originated in Edo (the historical name for Tokyo) during the 19th century, distinguished by techniques for treating fish with salt, vinegar, soy, and other preparations before service rather than serving it purely raw. The term encompasses ageing (allowing fish to develop umami depth over one to several days under controlled conditions), marinating (applying soy or citrus to adjust the flavour profile of specific cuts), searing (applying gentle heat to certain cuts to alter texture and aroma), and the use of red vinegar (akazu) in the shari rice blend to produce the distinctive darker, more complex rice seasoning characteristic of older Tokyo sushi houses. Sushi Kimura and Shinji by Kanesaka are the primary Edomae specialists in Singapore; both practice the full range of Edomae techniques rather than using the term as a marketing label for standard omakase.
