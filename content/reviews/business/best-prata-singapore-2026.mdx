Roti prata is as Singaporean as it is South Indian — a dish that arrived with Tamil Muslim migrants from southern India and the Indian subcontinent, absorbed into the daily rhythms of the island over more than a century, and now available at every hour of the day and night from hawker stalls, kopitiams, and dedicated prata restaurants from Jalan Kayu to Zam Zam. The name itself reflects the journey: "roti" from the Hindi and Urdu word for bread, and "prata" derived from the Hindi "paratha", meaning flat. In Malaysia, the equivalent dish is called roti canai — the two are essentially the same preparation, distinguished primarily by national pride rather than dough or technique. In Singapore, "roti prata" is the term that has stuck, and the standards to which the best stalls hold themselves are genuinely high.

The preparation is deceptively simple. A prata master works a ball of enriched dough — flour, water, ghee or oil, and sometimes condensed milk — stretching and folding it on an oiled marble surface until it becomes so thin as to be nearly transparent. The thin sheet is then folded back over itself to create layers, placed on a flat griddle greased with clarified butter, and cooked until the exterior achieves a crispiness that shatters at the first bite while the interior remains soft, chewy, and faintly steam-cooked from the folded layers. The accompaniment — a fish or mutton curry, sometimes dhal — is not incidental. It is the other half of the plate, and a stall with brilliant prata and mediocre curry is an incomplete experience.

We visited over 40 prata stalls across Singapore, ordering plain prata at each as the baseline test before expanding to egg, coin, murtabak, and tissue variants where the stall was known for them. The following is the most thorough ranking of Singapore roti prata in 2026.

## How We Ranked

We evaluated every stall on four primary criteria and two secondary factors:

**Dough quality** is the foundation. A well-made prata dough is enriched with ghee or oil to achieve extensibility, rested long enough for the gluten to relax, and worked on the griddle by someone with the wrist technique to stretch it to a thin, even sheet without tearing. Thick, doughy prata that has not been stretched properly produces a bready, heavy eating experience entirely different from the airy, layered structure that the preparation is capable of. We weighted dough quality heavily because it separates stalls where the maker has genuinely mastered the technique from those producing a competent but undistinguished product.

**Crispiness level** is the dimension most visible to the eater and most valued by the prata-eating community. The gold standard is a prata that shatters audibly when broken, with a lacquered, deeply golden exterior on both sides and a texture that maintains its structure for several minutes after leaving the griddle rather than going soft immediately. We penalised stalls where the prata emerged pale, soft, or unevenly cooked.

**Curry accompaniment** is the dimension most often overlooked in rankings that focus only on the prata itself. A house-made fish or mutton curry with real spice depth, proper coconut integration, and a texture that invites dipping rather than drowning the prata is a defining element of the full experience. Watery, underseasoned, or clearly commercial-mix curries dragged otherwise competent prata stalls down this list.

**Variety** was assessed for stalls known for their range — tissue prata, coin prata, murtabak, and stuffed variants — without penalising specialists who excel at plain and egg only.

**Value and consistency** rounded out the assessment. Given that plain prata costs SGD 1.20–2.00 at every stall on this list, value differences are marginal; consistency across multiple visits was the more meaningful secondary factor.

### 1. Mr and Mrs Mohgan's Super Crispy Roti Prata — Best Crispy Prata

<img src="/images/places/best-prata-singapore/mr-and-mrs-mohgan.jpg" alt="Mr and Mrs Mohgan" className="w-full rounded-lg my-4 object-cover" style={{height: "300px"}} />

<img src="/images/places/best-prata-singapore/mr-and-mrs-mohgans-super-crispy-roti-prata.jpg" alt="Mr and Mrs Mohgan's Super Crispy Roti Prata" className="w-full rounded-lg my-4 object-cover" style={{height: "300px"}} />

Mr and Mrs Mohgan's Super Crispy Roti Prata, operating from a modest morning stall on Crane Road in Joo Chiat, is the technical pinnacle of plain prata in Singapore — a stall where the maker's decades of practice have converged into a product whose crispiness, dough thinness, and structural precision no competitor on this list consistently surpasses. The prata that emerges from Mohgan's griddle is paper-thin, stretched to a near-translucent sheet before folding, producing a finished piece with a shatteringly crisp outer surface on both sides and an interior that remains soft and faintly chewy from the steam trapped between the folded layers. Breaking it reveals the layered structure that is the hallmark of a properly made prata — distinct strata rather than a compressed, doughy interior.

The fish curry served alongside is house-made with a depth of spicing that reflects years of refinement rather than a commercial base — mild enough to let the prata's own flavour register, but complex enough to make each dip worthwhile. Mr Mohgan himself is often still at the griddle in the early hours, and the speed and confidence of his technique is worth watching as a demonstration of what mastery looks like in a skill-dependent street food. The stall typically sells out by mid-morning on weekdays and earlier on weekends — arriving by 8am is the reliable strategy, and arriving after 10am on a Saturday carries a meaningful risk of finding the griddle cold.

The 24-hour operation is what truly distinguishes The Prata Shop from every other stall on this list. ### 3. Casuarina Curry Restaurant — Best Thin-Crispy Style

<img src="/images/places/best-prata-singapore/casuarina-curry-restaurant.jpg" alt="Casuarina Curry Restaurant" className="w-full rounded-lg my-4 object-cover" style={{height: "300px"}} />

Casuarina Curry Restaurant, at its original Casuarina Road location in the Thomson area, is the originator and most accomplished practitioner of what the prata community refers to as the "Casuarina style" — a prata stretched to extraordinary thinness, cooked until it achieves an almost lacquer-like crispiness across its entire surface, and served in pieces that shatter at the lightest pressure. The Casuarina style prioritises crispiness and thinness above all other qualities, producing a prata that is categorically different from the thicker, doughier styles found at many stalls: there is more surface area relative to interior, more browning relative to chew, and a texture that some experienced prata eaters regard as the purest expression of what the dish can be when the maker is willing to take the preparation to its structural limits.

The restaurant itself is a proper sit-down establishment rather than a hawker stall — air-conditioned, with table service and a full curry menu that extends well beyond the prata accompaniments to include mutton, chicken, and vegetable curries of genuine quality. The prata here is also available in egg and onion variants, both executed in the same thin-crispy style as the plain, and both worth ordering alongside the original. The Casuarina Road location is a pilgrimage site for the thin-crispy school of prata eating, and the 4.3-star rating on Google reflects a constituency of regulars who treat the specific texture of Casuarina prata as a non-negotiable standard.

Sin Ming Roti Prata, operating from its stall in the Sin Ming industrial estate since the 1980s, is the heritage prata stall that the serious prata-eating community returns to as a benchmark — a place where the original prata masters have continued their craft across four decades without the distractions of branding, expansion, or reinvention. The setting is characteristically unglamorous: a narrow stall in a utilitarian industrial block, a griddle that has been in continuous use since Reagan was president, and a queue that forms before the stall officially opens each morning. None of that matters once the prata arrives.

The Sin Ming plain prata is a study in correct technique applied consistently over decades: dough rested to the right elasticity, stretched to an even thinness without tearing, folded with the precision that produces even layers throughout the piece, and cooked on a well-seasoned surface at the temperature that produces deep golden-brown on both sides without burning. The fish curry at Sin Ming is outstanding — a complex, coconut-enriched preparation with a spice balance that reflects a recipe refined across forty years of daily service. The stall's 4.4-star Google rating is the highest of any heritage prata stall on this list and reflects a quality that has survived long enough to be recognised by a generation of diners who were not alive when Sin Ming opened.

### 5. Springleaf Prata Place — Best Variety

<img src="/images/places/best-prata-singapore/springleaf-prata-place.jpg" alt="Springleaf Prata Place" className="w-full rounded-lg my-4 object-cover" style={{height: "300px"}} />

Springleaf Prata Place, with its anchor outlet on Upper Thomson Road and additional locations across Singapore, occupies a distinctive position on this list as the stall with the most ambitious and successfully executed prata variety programme — a menu that extends from the traditional plain and egg through banana prata, chocolate prata, coin prata, and a range of stuffed variants that reward adventurous ordering without compromising the quality of the core plain prata. The thick prata style at Springleaf is a deliberate aesthetic choice rather than a technical shortcoming: the dough is laminated to a slightly greater thickness than the thin-crispy school, producing a prata with more chew in the interior and a softer outer texture that some diners prefer for its bread-like comfort.

The coin prata at Springleaf deserves specific mention — small, circular pieces cooked until uniformly golden and served with multiple curry dipping sauces, they function as a sharing platter and represent one of the most enjoyable ways to eat prata in a group setting on the island. The banana prata, a dessert variant, is well-executed: the sweetness of the banana integrates into the dough during cooking rather than sitting as a wet filling, and it is finished with a drizzle of condensed milk that makes it a legitimate dessert course. For families, groups with mixed preferences, or first-time visitors who want to sample the range of what prata can be in a single visit, Springleaf is the most practical recommendation on this list.

### 6. Zam Zam Restaurant — Best Murtabak

<img src="/images/places/best-prata-singapore/zam-zam-restaurant.jpg" alt="Zam Zam Restaurant" className="w-full rounded-lg my-4 object-cover" style={{height: "300px"}} />

Zam Zam Restaurant on Arab Street is the oldest establishment on this list — in operation since 1908, predating the formation of Malaysia, the Japanese occupation, Singapore's independence, and the entire contemporary food culture of the island. More than a century of continuous operation at the same address is itself a form of verdict on the quality of what is served here, and the murtabak at Zam Zam — a stuffed prata folded around a spiced minced beef or mutton filling with egg, onion, and aromatics, then cooked on the griddle until the exterior is crisp and the filling is cooked through — is the most historically significant plate of stuffed prata in Singapore and one of its most consistently excellent.

The Zam Zam murtabak is made to order, stuffed generously with a filling that is properly spiced rather than merely salted, and cooked on a griddle that has absorbed more history than most Singapore restaurants will ever accumulate. The beef murtabak is the flagship — a substantial piece that functions as a full meal — and the mutton version offers a richer, more pronounced flavour for those who prefer it. The plain prata at Zam Zam is good and honest, but the murtabak is the reason to make the Arab Street trip. The restaurant's 4.0-star Google rating understates the experience; Zam Zam's significance is historical as much as culinary, and its murtabak remains as close to the original preparation as anything available on the island.

### 10. Prata Wala — Best Modern Prata Chain

## Types of Prata: From Plain to Tissue

Singapore prata comes in a wider range of forms than the plain-and-egg binary that most casual diners encounter. Understanding the variety available is the first step toward getting more from a prata visit.

**Plain prata** (kosong) is the baseline and the truest test of a prata maker's skill. With no filling to hide behind, a plain prata lives or dies on the quality of its dough, the correctness of its stretch, and the temperature control on the griddle. This is what we ordered first at every stall on this list.

**Egg prata** adds a cracked egg to the dough surface before the final fold and cooking, producing a prata with a cooked egg integrated into the interior. When made well, the egg remains slightly soft in the centre. When made carelessly, it overcooks into a rubbery layer. A good egg prata is one of the most satisfying hawker breakfasts in Singapore.

**Cheese prata** adds processed cheese to the interior — a concession to modern preferences that nevertheless works when the cheese is present in sufficient quantity and melts properly during cooking. It is popular with children and with diners who find plain prata too subtle.

**Onion prata** folds chopped yellow onion into the dough before cooking, producing a prata with a savoury, slightly sweet edge from the caramelised onion and a textural contrast between the crisp exterior and soft onion interior.

**Coin prata** comprises small, circular pieces — typically six to eight per serving — produced from the same dough and cooked on the griddle until uniformly golden. They are designed for sharing and for dipping into multiple curry varieties simultaneously, making them the ideal format for group visits.

**Banana prata** and other dessert variants fold fresh banana (and sometimes chocolate or cheese) into the dough, creating a sweet prata that functions as a dessert course rather than a savoury dish. Springleaf Prata Place executes these well; at lesser stalls, the wet fruit filling prevents the exterior from crisping.

**Murtabak** is a stuffed variant of its own — a thicker, larger piece of dough filled with spiced minced beef or mutton, egg, and onion, folded and pressed on the griddle until the exterior is crisp and the filling is fully cooked. It is closer to a stuffed flatbread than a layered prata, and at its best — Zam Zam's version being the standard — it is one of the most satisfying hawker dishes in Singapore.

**Tissue prata** (also called paper prata) is the most technically demanding variant: the dough is stretched to near-transparent thinness across the entire griddle surface, folded into a tall cone or tree shape, and cooked until the structure holds and the surface achieves a glass-like crispiness. It is sprinkled with sugar and condensed milk and eaten as a dessert piece. The stretching and shaping require a dexterity that not all prata makers possess — stalls that offer tissue prata without the skill to execute it properly produce a limp, collapsed disappointment.

## How to Judge a Great Prata

After tasting prata at more than 40 stalls, three elements reliably distinguish excellent prata from merely acceptable:

**Dough quality and technique.** The most important non-negotiable. Correct prata dough is enriched with ghee to give it extensibility, rested sufficiently for the gluten to relax, and worked on the griddle by a maker who can stretch it to a consistent, even thinness without tearing. The tell-tale signs of well-made dough are: a prata that maintains distinct layers when broken open, an interior that is soft without being doughy or dense, and an exterior that achieves genuine crispiness rather than mere browning. Dough that has been under-rested tears during stretching; dough that is too thick produces a bready, heavy eating experience.

**Cooking temperature and timing.** Prata cooked on an insufficiently hot griddle produces a pale, soft result without the Maillard-reaction browning that generates the crisp texture. Prata cooked at too high a temperature burns the exterior before the layers cook through. The correct griddle temperature — and the maker's ability to read and adjust it in real time, given the variation in ghee coverage, dough thickness, and ambient temperature — is a skill that separates trained prata makers from those working from muscle memory alone. Watch the colour: a properly cooked prata is a deep, even golden-brown on both sides, not pale and soft on one side with a darker patch where the griddle ran hotter.

**The curry.** A house-made fish or mutton curry that complements rather than overwhelms the prata is the difference between a complete meal and a plate that is merely technically correct. The best curries on this list — Sin Ming's fish curry, Haji M Abdul Rajak's mutton curry, Mohgan's fish curry — have been refined across decades of daily preparation and reflect a depth of spicing and a balance of heat, coconut richness, and acidity that commercial mix curries cannot replicate. If the curry smells like a processed base rather than freshly bloomed spices, the stall has taken a shortcut that the prata's quality rarely survives.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is roti prata?

Roti prata is a South Indian-influenced flatbread common in Singapore and Malaysia (where it is called roti canai). It is made from an enriched dough of flour, water, ghee, and sometimes condensed milk, stretched thin on an oiled surface, folded to create layers, and cooked on a flat griddle in clarified butter until crispy on the outside and soft inside. It is typically served with fish curry, mutton curry, or dhal for dipping. The dish arrived in Singapore with Tamil Muslim migrants and has been a staple of the island's hawker food culture for over a century.

### Where is the best prata in Singapore?

Mr and Mrs Mohgan's Super Crispy Roti Prata on Crane Road in Joo Chiat is the best plain prata in Singapore by the crispiness and dough-thinness standard that most serious prata eaters apply. For 24-hour access, The Prata Shop on Upper Thomson Road is unmatched. For the thinnest possible crispy style, Casuarina Curry Restaurant on Casuarina Road is the benchmark. For murtabak specifically, Zam Zam Restaurant on Arab Street has been the standard since 1908. For heritage stall prata, Sin Ming Roti Prata is the recommendation.

### How much does prata cost in Singapore?

A plain prata (kosong) at a hawker stall costs SGD 1.20–1.80 in 2026. Egg prata runs SGD 1.80–2.50. Stuffed variants like cheese or onion prata are typically SGD 2.00–3.00. Murtabak starts at SGD 8–12 depending on filling and size. Tissue prata is typically SGD 5–8. At restaurant settings like Casuarina Curry and Springleaf Prata Place, prices are slightly higher but remain in the SGD 2–4 range for plain and egg variants. Prata represents some of the best value for money in Singapore's hawker food scene at any price point.

### What is murtabak?

Murtabak is a stuffed variant of prata — a larger, thicker piece of dough filled with spiced minced beef or mutton, egg, and onion, folded and pressed flat on the griddle until the exterior is crispy and the filling is fully cooked through. It functions as a substantial meal rather than a snack, and the best versions — Zam Zam's beef murtabak being the historic benchmark — have a properly spiced filling, a crisp exterior, and a dipping curry that completes the plate. Murtabak is also found in Malaysia and Indonesia, where regional variations exist in the filling spicing and dough thickness.

### What time do prata stalls open in Singapore?

Most traditional prata stalls open between 6am and 8am for the breakfast trade and serve through the morning until sell-out — typically between 10am and 1pm for the most popular stalls like Mr and Mrs Mohgan's and Sin Ming. The Prata Shop on Upper Thomson is open 24 hours and is the most notable exception. Many stalls also offer evening service from around 6pm until midnight or later, making prata one of Singapore's most accessible late-night food options. The 24-hour accessibility of prata in Singapore, through stalls like The Prata Shop and various Jalan Kayu operators, is a defining feature of how the dish functions in the city's daily food culture.

## Verdict

Mr and Mrs Mohgan's Super Crispy Roti Prata in Joo Chiat is the most technically accomplished plain prata in Singapore in 2026 — a stall where four decades of practice have produced a product whose crispiness, dough thinness, and structural precision no competitor consistently surpasses. The trip to Crane Road is worth making from anywhere on the island, and arriving by 8am is the only reliable strategy for getting prata before sell-out. For 24-hour access to genuinely good traditional prata, The Prata Shop on Upper Thomson is without peer — open since 1974, available at 2am when nothing else is, and consistent at a level most 24-hour operations cannot sustain. Casuarina Curry Restaurant owns the thin-crispy aesthetic and has defined a style so specific that it now bears the restaurant's name. Sin Ming carries the flame of the heritage industrial estate stall, unchanged and excellent across four decades. Zam Zam on Arab Street is the murtabak standard by historical consensus and by continued quality. Together, these five represent the range of what roti prata in Singapore can be — and the gap between the best of them and an undifferentiated hawker centre plain is wide enough to justify planning your visits with the same care you would apply to any other great Singapore food experience.
