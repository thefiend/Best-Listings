## What to Look for in an Omakase Experience

**Chef credentials and training lineage.** The quality of an omakase experience is almost entirely a function of the chef behind the counter. Look for verifiable training under a recognised Japanese master, ideally with experience working in Japan or at a well-regarded Singapore institution before opening their own counter. Counters that do not publish any chef background information should be treated with caution.

**Review volume and recency.** A 4.9-star rating on 50 reviews is not the same as a 4.9-star rating on 400 reviews. Volume indicates consistency across many sittings and many different diners. Recency matters too: check that reviews include dates from the past three months to confirm the counter has not changed chefs or declined in quality since the peak of its reputation.

**Sourcing transparency.** The best omakase counters in Singapore import fish from Japan at least twice weekly, often referencing specific markets such as Toyosu or seasonal regions in Hokkaido or Kyushu. Counters that are vague about sourcing, or that combine Japanese and non-Japanese fish without disclosure, are likely operating at a lower ingredient tier than their pricing implies.

**Menu format and progression.** A true omakase progression moves from lighter, more delicate preparations through to richer, more intense courses, ending with a composed dessert or wagashi. Counters that skip the appetiser progression and jump directly into nigiri, or that serve a fixed set menu with no chef discretion, are delivering a set meal rather than a true omakase experience.

**Booking process and transparency.** Reputable counters operate transparent booking systems with clear pricing, cancellation policies, and availability. Instagram DM booking is common for smaller counters and is not inherently problematic, but the pricing and deposit structure should be confirmed in writing before your visit. Counters that quote prices only on arrival or that have no posted pricing should raise questions about how they handle disputes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does an omakase meal cost in Singapore?

Omakase meals in Singapore range from approximately SGD 80 to SGD 600 per person. Entry-level counters such as Gary's Omakase at Hong Lim Complex operate at the lower end, around SGD 80 to SGD 150. Mid-range counters in Tanjong Pagar typically price at SGD 150 to SGD 300. Premium counters with imported Japanese seasonal produce and multiple courses price between SGD 300 and SGD 600 per person.

### How far in advance should I book an omakase in Singapore?

Most highly rated omakase counters in Singapore require 4 to 8 weeks of advance booking. Takemitsu and Honō frequently fill 8 to 10 weeks ahead, particularly on Friday and Saturday evenings. For weekday sittings at less prominent counters such as Sushi Katori, 2 to 3 weeks may be sufficient. Booking via a structured reservation platform like OpenTable is faster than Instagram DM enquiries.

### What is the difference between omakase and kaiseki?

Omakase refers to a meal where the diner places themselves entirely in the chef's hands, typically a sushi-focused progression in Singapore. Kaiseki is a formal multi-course Japanese meal structure with defined course categories including sakizuke, hassun, yakimono, and others, often featuring both cooked and raw preparations. Many Singapore omakase counters blend elements of both formats, particularly in the appetiser and cooked course sections.

### What should I expect at a Singapore omakase counter?

Expect a counter seating arrangement facing the chef, a multi-course progression lasting 90 to 180 minutes, and a set price per person with limited substitution options. Most counters include sake and tea pairings at additional cost. Conversation with the chef is expected and welcomed. Dietary restrictions should be disclosed at booking, not on the day of your visit.

### Are omakase restaurants in Singapore regulated or licensed?
