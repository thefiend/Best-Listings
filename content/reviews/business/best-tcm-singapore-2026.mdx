Traditional Chinese Medicine has occupied a distinctive place in Singapore's healthcare ecosystem for well over a century. What began as a community-based practice serving the city-state's early Chinese immigrant population has evolved into a formally regulated, government-integrated component of Singapore's national health system. Today, the Ministry of Health (MOH) registers TCM practitioners under the Traditional Chinese Medicine Practitioners Act, and the TCM Practitioners Board (TCMPB) maintains a statutory register of all licensed practitioners. Only practitioners appearing on the TCMPB register are legally permitted to practise acupuncture, prescribe Chinese proprietary medicines, or perform other regulated TCM modalities in Singapore. When evaluating any TCM clinic in Singapore, verifying that the treating physician holds a current TCMPB registration is the non-negotiable first step — not an optional courtesy.

The demand profile for TCM in Singapore is broad and continues to grow. Musculoskeletal conditions — lower back pain, neck pain, sports injuries, arthritis, and post-surgical recovery — represent the largest share of TCM clinic bookings. Women's health conditions, including menstrual irregularities, fertility support, and perimenopausal symptoms, form a significant and growing segment, particularly at clinics that have invested in dedicated women's health TCM programmes. Stress-related conditions, chronic fatigue, insomnia, and digestive disorders reflect the urban health burden of Singapore's working population. Cancer supportive care — palliative symptom management and treatment-side-effect mitigation — is an emerging area where TCM is increasingly integrated with oncology departments at restructured hospitals.

Singapore's regulatory framework for TCM is among the most rigorous in the region. All practising TCM physicians must hold either a degree from a TCM institution recognised by the TCMPB or have passed the TCMPB competency examinations. Continuous professional development requirements are enforced. Several restructured hospitals — including Singapore General Hospital, National University Hospital, and Raffles Hospital — offer TCM services integrated with Western medicine, reflecting the mainstreaming of TCM as a complement to, rather than a substitute for, conventional medical care. For patients navigating serious health conditions, this integrated model, where TCM physicians and Western doctors can review the same patient, represents the safest and most evidence-aligned approach to TCM care.

MediShield Life does not currently cover TCM outpatient consultations. Medisave can be used for specific inpatient TCM treatments in MOH-licensed hospitals, but outpatient TCM remains largely a private out-of-pocket expenditure for most Singaporeans. Several insurers and corporate health benefit schemes include TCM riders or allowances, and some clinics accept selected health insurance panels. Session costs for private TCM range from approximately SGD 30–80 for a consultation with herbal prescription, to SGD 60–150 for acupuncture sessions, depending on the clinic's positioning, the physician's seniority, and the treatment modalities employed.

## How We Ranked These TCM Clinics

Our methodology for ranking the best TCM clinic in Singapore 2026 applied the following criteria in order of weight. TCMPB registration: only clinics where every treating TCM physician holds a current TCM Practitioners Board registration were considered — this is the legal baseline and the primary patient safety filter. Physician qualifications and experience: the academic credentials of the treating physicians, post-graduate specialisation, and years of clinical experience were assessed where disclosed. Treatment range: clinics offering the full spectrum of regulated TCM modalities — acupuncture, herbal medicine, tui na, cupping, moxibustion, and gua sha — were weighted above single-modality providers. Clinic hygiene and standards: clinical environment quality, equipment sterilisation practices for acupuncture, and MOH-compliant facility standards were assessed. Google star ratings: a minimum of 4.0 stars was required for inclusion, with higher ratings rewarded. Accessibility: clinic network breadth, MRT proximity, and appointment availability. Integration with Western medicine: clinics operating within hospital systems or with formal referral relationships with Western medical providers were weighted for patient safety in complex cases. All data reflects conditions as of August 2026.

## TCM Treatments in Singapore: What Each Does

Understanding what each TCM modality involves helps patients choose the right clinic for their specific needs and have an informed conversation with their TCMPB-registered physician.

**Acupuncture** involves the insertion of sterile, single-use fine needles at specific acupuncture points along meridian pathways to stimulate Qi flow, reduce pain, and regulate physiological function. Acupuncture is the most extensively researched TCM modality and carries strong clinical evidence for pain management — lower back pain, neck pain, headache, osteoarthritis — as well as for nausea management and certain neurological presentations. In Singapore, acupuncture must be performed by a TCMPB-registered physician. Session duration typically ranges from 20–45 minutes of needle retention after insertion. Sterile single-use needles are the MOH standard and any clinic that cannot confirm this practice should not be used.

**Chinese Herbal Medicine** (CHM) involves the prescription of individualised herbal formulas — combinations of plant, mineral, and animal-derived substances classified according to TCM pharmacology — prepared as decoctions (boiled teas), granulated powders, or patent pills. A competent CHM prescription is highly individualised, taking into account the patient's TCM pattern diagnosis, constitution, season, and any concurrent Western medications. Drug-herb interaction screening is a critical physician responsibility, particularly for patients on anticoagulants, immunosuppressants, or cardiovascular medications. Only TCMPB-registered physicians may prescribe regulated Chinese proprietary medicines in Singapore.

**Tui Na** is a system of therapeutic manual manipulation applied along meridians and acupuncture points using specific hand techniques — pushing, rolling, kneading, pressing, and joint mobilisation — to relieve muscular tension, improve circulation, and address musculoskeletal pain. Tui na is distinct from general relaxation massage: it is a clinical intervention requiring training in TCM anatomy, meridian theory, and pathology. It is particularly effective for neck and shoulder pain, lower back conditions, and as a component of sports injury rehabilitation.

**Cupping** places glass or silicone cups on the skin to create suction, drawing blood and lymph to the surface and releasing deep fascial tension. Used for respiratory conditions, muscular pain, and improving circulation, cupping typically leaves circular red-purple marks (petechiae) that resolve within a week. It is commonly combined with acupuncture or tui na in TCM clinical practice.

**Moxibustion** applies heat to acupuncture points using burning moxa (dried mugwort) — either directly on the skin or indirectly via needles or salt — to warm meridians, dispel cold, and strengthen Yang Qi. Moxibustion is particularly used for cold-pattern conditions: digestive disorders aggravated by cold, painful joints worsened by cool weather, fatigue associated with cold constitution, and turning breech babies in late pregnancy. The distinctive herbal smoke of moxibustion is immediately recognisable and not suitable for patients with respiratory sensitivities.

**Gua Sha** involves pressing a smooth-edged tool firmly along the skin surface to produce petechiae (sha), improving microcirculation, releasing fascial adhesions, and addressing stagnation patterns associated with pain and restricted movement. Gua sha is used for neck and upper back pain, respiratory illness, and fever management in the acute stage. Like cupping, it produces characteristic marks that are diagnostically meaningful in TCM and resolve within a few days.

## How to Check If Your TCM Practitioner Is MOH-Registered

Verifying your TCM physician's TCMPB registration before your first appointment is the single most important patient safety action in Singapore's TCM sector. The process is straightforward.

Visit the TCM Practitioners Board official website at **tcmpb.gov.sg** and navigate to the Register of TCM Practitioners. The register is publicly searchable by the practitioner's name and/or registration number. A valid registration will show the practitioner's full name, registration number, registration type (acupuncturist, TCM physician, or both), and registration status (active or suspended).

Registration types in Singapore are important to understand. A TCMPB-registered TCM Physician is qualified to practise the full scope of TCM, including prescribing Chinese proprietary medicines. A TCMPB-registered Acupuncturist is qualified specifically for acupuncture practice. A practitioner who performs acupuncture without TCMPB registration as either a TCM physician or acupuncturist is operating illegally under the Traditional Chinese Medicine Practitioners Act, regardless of any claimed qualifications or overseas credentials.

If a clinic declines to provide the TCMPB registration number of the treating physician, that is a significant red flag. Any legitimate TCM clinic in Singapore should display their physicians' registration numbers at the clinic and be willing to confirm them on request. Do not attend any TCM clinic that cannot or will not confirm its practitioners' TCMPB registration status.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is Traditional Chinese Medicine and how does it work?**

Traditional Chinese Medicine is a comprehensive system of medicine developed over more than 2,500 years in China, based on the theory that health is maintained by the balanced flow of vital energy (Qi) through a network of meridians in the body. TCM practitioners diagnose health conditions by assessing patterns of imbalance — using the four examinations of looking, listening/smelling, asking, and touching (including pulse and tongue diagnosis) — and treat them through interventions that restore balance, including acupuncture, herbal medicine, tui na massage, cupping, moxibustion, and dietary guidance. In Singapore, TCM is formally regulated by the Ministry of Health, and TCM practitioners must hold TCMPB registration to practice legally. TCM is increasingly used as a complement to Western medicine rather than an alternative, particularly for pain management, chronic disease support, and women's health conditions.

**Is TCM covered by MediShield Life in Singapore?**

MediShield Life does not currently cover outpatient TCM consultations in Singapore. Medisave can be used for specific inpatient TCM treatments provided in MOH-licensed hospitals, but this applies to a relatively small subset of TCM care. Most outpatient TCM — consultations, herbal prescriptions, acupuncture, tui na — is paid out of pocket or through private health insurance riders and corporate health benefit plans. Several major insurers offer TCM riders or include TCM benefits in integrated shield plans, and some clinics are accredited on corporate insurance panels. Always confirm the payment options and insurance compatibility with your chosen clinic before booking. Government polyclinic TCM services (such as SingHealth Polyclinic TCM) are accessible at subsidised rates for eligible Singapore Citizens and Permanent Residents, providing the most cost-effective access to MOH-registered TCM care.

**How much does acupuncture cost in Singapore?**

Acupuncture session fees at private TCM clinics in Singapore typically range from SGD 60–150 per session depending on the clinic's positioning, the treating physician's seniority, session duration, and the number of needles and techniques used. An initial assessment visit at a specialist TCM clinic may cost SGD 80–200, as the physician conducts a thorough diagnostic evaluation before commencing treatment. At government polyclinic TCM services, acupuncture is available at subsidised rates that can be significantly lower for eligible Singapore Citizens. Standalone acupuncture treatment typically takes 45–75 minutes including the consultation, insertion, needle retention period, and removal. Multiple-session packages at private clinics often offer a small discount on per-session pricing. For patients comparing clinics, a lower price for acupuncture should be scrutinised: the legal requirement for TCMPB registration means that very low-cost "acupuncture" offerings at wellness centres or beauty salons not staffed by registered practitioners may be operating outside the law.

**What conditions does TCM treat effectively?**

TCM carries the strongest clinical evidence for musculoskeletal pain management — lower back pain, neck pain, osteoarthritis, shoulder pain, and headache — where acupuncture in particular has been validated in multiple systematic reviews and clinical trials. Good evidence also supports TCM for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, chronic fatigue, and certain aspects of women's reproductive health including cycle regulation and IVF support. TCM is widely used for stress, anxiety, insomnia, digestive disorders, respiratory conditions (particularly chronic sinusitis and allergic rhinitis), and post-stroke rehabilitation. TCM physicians in Singapore also provide constitutional health maintenance programmes for patients without specific diagnoses who seek general wellness optimisation. For serious medical conditions — malignancy, infectious disease, cardiovascular emergencies, surgical emergencies — TCM should be considered a complement to, not a substitute for, emergency Western medical care. Always disclose all TCM treatments and herbal prescriptions to your Western medical practitioners to prevent drug-herb interactions.

**Do I need a referral to see a TCM physician in Singapore?**

You do not need a doctor's referral to book an appointment at a private TCM clinic in Singapore. TCM physicians are autonomous practitioners registered under the TCMPB, and you can contact any private TCM clinic directly to schedule a consultation. A referral is only required if you wish to access government polyclinic-linked TCM services at subsidised rates, which typically involves a polyclinic appointment first. For patients seeking TCM within a hospital-integrated system such as Raffles Chinese Medicine or Thomson Chinese Medicine, a self-referral to the TCM service is generally possible, though hospital-based TCM services may have specific booking processes. That said, if your TCM need arises from a condition already being managed by a Western physician — particularly for patients on multiple medications or with complex diagnoses — informing both your Western doctor and your TCM physician about each other's involvement is strongly recommended to enable safe, coordinated care.
